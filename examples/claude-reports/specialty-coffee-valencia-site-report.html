The recommendation

Specialty coffee is still the growth segment inside a large Spanish café market. Branded coffee shops grew for a third year running, by 3% to 2,215 stores in the year to April 2025, and about a third of the 67 million cups drunk in Spain each day are drunk away from home.

In València the premises side is cheap: 40–120 m² retail units ask a median of €900 a month and 98% ask €3,500 or less. The constraint is trade, not rent. A takeaway bar lives on the queue before 11 am and on regulars, so we scored each site on morning and local footfall first, then catchment, spending power, the unit, café competition and cost.

Open La Petxina first as the neighbourhood unit: the lowest rent, the strongest morning and local trade, and a finished take-away fit-out, with its licence still in process. Take Russafa as the visible flagship: a corner with ten metres of glass and a definitive licence, in the city’s most contested café quarter. Sant Francesc is a volume play for a second phase; the other three do not suit a takeaway format.

The specialty coffee sweet spot

A specialty coffee bar sells a small ticket many times a day. It needs to be on the morning route of people who come back, not on a tourist street or in a bar quarter.

The specialty coffee customer

Specialty coffee in Spain is bought as an affordable treat rather than as fuel, and once people switch they rarely go back to commodity coffee. That makes the habit, and the route it sits on, more valuable than raw footfall: a site that catches the same 300 people every weekday morning beats one that sees thousands of visitors once.

Calle Mestre Guerrero 5, La Petxina

Freshly refurbished take-away café, business transfer, licence for bakery and take-away coffee in process
Lead: neighbourhood unit

Built as a take-away café and just refitted, this unit is the closest thing in the screen to a ready specialty bar. It has the strongest morning and local trade of any busy site: 30% of footfall before noon and 77% from within 3 km, with weekends 30% quieter, the signature of a residential weekday neighbourhood. Thirty-two thousand people live within a ten-minute walk and only five cafés trade within five minutes.

The trade-offs are scale and income: footfall is below the city median, spending power is average, and the licence is still being processed. The neighbourhood is moving up, with homes nearby asking €3,444/m² and registered sales up 19% this year.

Plaza Barón de Cortes, Russafa

Corner bar-cafetería beside the Russafa market, business transfer
Lead: flagship

The balanced site: a third of a million on the footfall index with almost no weekend drop, six in ten passers-by local, and 59% of nearby households above €40,000. The unit is a corner with ten metres of glass, an open floor, extraction and a definitive licence, at a rent just under the Russafa median.

The cost of that visibility is competition. Twelve cafés and 56 restaurants trade within five minutes, and the busiest part of the day here is the evening, which suits bars more than coffee.

Risks and next steps

Risks and assumptions

  • All six are business transfers; key money is unstated except at Malvarrosa.
  • Footfall and demographics are 2024 modelled data; café counts are OpenStreetMap places.
  • La Petxina’s licence is in process and its exact address is withheld.
  • Asking rents are rising about 12% a year, which favours longer lease terms.

Next steps

  1. Visit La Petxina and Russafa at 7:30 and 10:30 on a weekday to count the morning flow.
  2. Get transfer fees for both and a licence status letter for La Petxina.
  3. Run a second screen in Benimaclet, the university quarter with the most supply at €10/m².
  4. Re-score with the operator’s own sales mix by daypart.
